Match accounts to people and report your own match rate

Depends on: Sub-task 8 — you need the measured shapes and the 1200-vs-1188 anomaly Time: ≈ 75 min Type: reconciliation · the hard join
First appearance
identity reconciliation orphan account

This is the single hardest step in a real user access review, and the one clients most often assume is easy.

The entitlements export identifies people by account_name. The HR roster identifies them by employee_id. There is no column in common. Building the bridge between them is called identity reconciliation, and everything downstream is worthless without it.

You cannot review access for a person you cannot identify. That sounds obvious, and it is exactly the step that gets waved through in practice — someone joins on name, gets a number, and nobody asks what happened to the rows that didn't join. This sub-task is about being the person who asks.

What you'll understand and do

  • Explain why these two exports share no key, and what that forces you to do
  • Direct Claude to build a name matcher, and inspect the pile it fails on
  • State your match rate as a number, and defend the accounts you left unmatched

Concept

The one idea

The rows that don't join are the interesting ones.

Here is what you're joining. From entitlements.csv:

entitlements.csv 
account_id,account_name,app,role,granted_date,...
A000832,brett.fleming,Active Directory,Standard,2026-03-06,...

And from hr_roster.csv:

hr_roster.csv 
employee_id,full_name,email,department,title,hire_date,...
E00086,Jenna Fleming,jenna.fleming@meridian-energy.com,Engineering,...

A000832 and E00086 have nothing to do with each other. The only bridge is a human name, written one way in one system and another way in the other. So the join key has to be derived, and a derived key is a key that can be wrong.

A shortcut that does not exist — check for yourself

An earlier build of this export had a generator bug: the digits in account_id happened to line up with the digits in employee_id, so A000251 belonged to E00251. A student who noticed could skip this entire sub-task.

That bug is fixed. In the export you have, 0 of the 1,073 name-matched accounts also match on digits. Verify that yourself before you trust me — it takes one line, and "check whether the convenient thing is true" is the habit this course is actually teaching.

Three ways a name fails to join, and they need three different answers:

1
Format variants — recoverable

cmckee, taylorc, natalieg. A real person with an account named in an older convention.

Answer: widen the matcher, carefully.

2
Collisions — ambiguous, must not be guessed

Two different employees share a name slug. Joining on name would attach one person's access to the other's record.

Answer: resolve to nobody, and mark why.

3
Orphans — genuinely nobody

An account with no human behind it at all. In an access review this is not a data problem, it is a finding.

Answer: keep it, flag it, never drop it.

The failure mode to avoid is collapsing all three into "didn't match, ignore." That single decision is how a real review misses the account belonging to nobody that still has production access.

Task 1

Build the baseline matcher and get your first number

  1. First, disprove the shortcut.

    Why: because I told you it doesn't work, and that isn't evidence. Compare the numeric part of account_id with the numeric part of the employee_id you'd get from matching on name. Count how many agree.

    expected output 
    accounts whose A-number digits equal their person's E-number digits: 0 / 1073
  2. Direct Claude to build an exact firstname.lastname matcher, and to classify every account rather than only the ones that work.

    Why: the classification is the deliverable. State the intent yourself — but the output has to distinguish four outcomes, not two: matched (exactly one person), ambiguous (more than one person fits), service (listed in data/2026-Q3/service_accounts.json), unmatched (nobody fits). All 1,200 accounts must land in exactly one bucket.

    The HR side has a usable slug already: the local part of the email address, before the @. Deriving it from full_name works too and is a good check on both.

  3. Check the baseline against these numbers.
    expected output 
    exact firstname.lastname matching
  matched      1055
  ambiguous      18
  service         5
  unmatched     122
  ------------------
  total        1200      match rate 87.9%

    If you get 1073 matched and no ambiguous bucket, your matcher is silently picking a winner when two people fit. That's not a rounding error — it's 18 accounts attributed to the wrong human. Task 3 is about exactly this.

Stuck?

I get 1073 matched. That's the count of accounts whose name slug appears in HR at all. Eighteen of those slugs belong to two employees each. Group HR by slug first and check the group size before resolving.

My total isn't 1200. You're probably counting entitlement rows rather than accounts. There are 15,188 rows and 1,200 distinct account_id values. De-duplicate to one row per account first.

Where does 87.9% come from? 1055 / 1200. Whether that's the right denominator is a genuine question — you could argue the five known service accounts shouldn't count against you. Pick one, say which you picked, and be consistent. Auditors care far more about the second part than the first.

Task 2

Read the unmatched pile with your own eyes

  1. Print all 122 unmatched account names and actually look at them.

    Why: 122 rows is small enough to read and large enough to have structure. This is the one moment in the theme where the right tool is your eyes. Do not ask Claude to summarize the list for you — you are trying to notice something, and a summary is the opposite of noticing.

    a sample of what you'll see 
    cmckee        taylorc       natalieg      jburnett      eugenem
kendra.brown  john.richards jill.smith    leah.lee      todd.hudson
marcus.pipeline             emergency.admin

    Two shapes should jump out. Names with no dot look like an older username convention. Names with a dot look like real people who simply aren't in the roster — a very different problem.

  2. Widen the matcher to cover the initial-style variants — and keep the ambiguity rule.

    Why: cmckee is plausibly C. McKee; taylorc is plausibly Taylor C. Both patterns are worth handling. Both also collide far more often than full names do, so a variant that fits two people must land in ambiguous, not in matched.

    expected output — after widening 
    + first-initial + lastname  (cmckee)   and  firstname + last-initial  (taylorc)

  matched      1118      (+63)
  ambiguous      32      (+14)
  service         5
  unmatched      45      (-77)
  ------------------
  total        1200      match rate 93.2%

    Note what happened to ambiguous: it grew. A wider matcher does not only find more people, it finds more ways to be unsure — and a matcher that never grows its ambiguous bucket is one that's quietly guessing.

  3. Look at what's left. Those 45 are the real answer.

    Why: forty of them are ordinary firstname.lastname names — kendra.brown, jill.smith, todd.hudson — that no widening will ever reach, because there is no such person in HR. Those are orphan accounts: live credentials with no owner. In a user access review that is a finding in its own right, and one of the more serious ones.

Stuck?

My widened numbers are higher than 1118. Almost always the ambiguity check. There are 98 distinct first-initial+lastname keys in HR that fit more than one employee. If a variant match resolves to two different employee_id values, it is not a match.

Should I go further — fuzzy matching, edit distance, nicknames? You can, and in a real engagement you might. Two cautions. Every widening buys matches at the price of false attributions, so measure both. And the remaining 45 in this data are not near-misses; they have no counterpart at any distance.

Task 3

Refuse to merge the collisions

  1. Find the HR name slugs owned by more than one employee.

    Why: there are 10 of them, and they account for the 18 ambiguous accounts in your baseline. This is the mechanism behind the 1,200-vs-1,188 anomaly you noticed in sub-task 8.

  2. Check the worked case: jacob.miller.

    Why: accounts A000450 and A001148 are both named jacob.miller, and they belong to two different employees. A join on name attaches both accounts to whichever Jacob sorts first, and the other Jacob's access silently vanishes from the review. The kit tests this case by name.

    The correct handling is to resolve neither. An unresolved account that says so is honest; a resolved account that's wrong is worse than useless, because it looks reviewed.

  3. Handle the service accounts — including the two that look human.

    Why: data/2026-Q3/service_accounts.json lists five names. Three are obvious: svc-atlas-batch, svc-backup-runner, svc-gateway-sync. Two are not: marcus.pipeline and emergency.admin look exactly like people, and marcus.pipeline owns three separate accounts.

    Any rule of the shape "names starting with svc- are service accounts" will misclassify both. Read the file; don't infer from the string. A service account must resolve to no human owner — the kit tests that too.
