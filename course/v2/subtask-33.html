Judge the rationales, not the verdicts

Depends on: Sub-task 32 — you need a full run with escalations to grade Time: ≈ 75 min Type: evals · LLM-as-judge
First appearance
LLM-as-judge rubric defensibility

The eval that needs no answer key at all — because it grades the argument, not the conclusion.

The micro-key covers twelve accounts. Your quarter has nine findings and a review queue, and most of them aren't in the key. You have no way to assess them.

Except you do, if you change the question. Instead of is this verdict correct? — which needs an answer key — ask is this verdict defensible? Is the evidence real, does the citation exist, does the reasoning actually follow?

That question needs no key. It can be answered from the finding itself, which means it scales to every finding you will ever produce.

What you'll understand and do

  • Explain why defensibility is assessable without ground truth
  • Write a rubric with criteria specific enough to be scored consistently
  • Score every finding's rationale and read the worst three
  • Know where LLM-as-judge is reliable and where it isn't

Concept

The one idea

You can't check the answer. You can check the working.

An access review is judged by an auditor who wasn't there. They pull three findings and ask you to justify them. What they're assessing is not whether you reached the objectively correct conclusion — they can't know that either — but whether the finding is defensible: grounded in stated evidence, tied to a real clause, and reasoned in a way that follows.

That is a property of the finding as written. It requires no external truth, which is precisely why it scales where the micro-key doesn't.

What is LLM-as-judge?

A second model call that scores an output against a rubric. It is not the same model marking its own homework — it is a separate call, with a different prompt, given the artifact and asked specific questions about it.

It works well for assessable properties: does every claim trace to stated evidence, is the citation among the legitimate ones, does the severity match the stated impact. It works badly for correctness, because a judge with no access to ground truth has no more idea than the original whether the answer is right.

Keeping that line clear is the difference between a useful eval and a very expensive way of generating agreement.

The rubric is the whole artifact. "Rate this finding's quality from 1 to 5" produces noise — different runs score the same finding differently because there's nothing to anchor to. Specific, checkable criteria produce something reproducible.

Criteria that actually discriminate

Evidence grounding. Does every factual claim in the rationale appear in the evidence array? A rationale asserting the account was unused for ten months, with no last_login in evidence, fails.

Citation validity. Is the citation one of the ten legitimate identifiers from sub-task 16? This one you should check in code as well — it's deterministic, and a judge is a wasteful way to do string matching.

Citation relevance. Does the cited clause govern this control? ACP-4.2 on a dormancy finding is a real clause attached to the wrong thing, and this is where a judge earns its cost because code can't assess it.

Reasoning validity. Does the conclusion follow from the evidence and the clause, or is there a step missing?

Severity justification. Is the severity argued rather than asserted?

Exemption consideration. If a relevant exemption exists, is it addressed — even to rule it out? A dormancy finding that never mentions break-glass has skipped a step.

Score each criterion separately. A single blended number tells you a finding is weak; six criteria tell you which part is weak, which is the difference between a metric and a diagnostic.

Do this

Task 1

Write the rubric

  1. Write it as a file, not as a prompt string.

    Why: it's a specification of what a good finding looks like, and it belongs in version control where it can be reviewed and argued with. It will also outlive this pipeline — a rubric is transferable in a way that a prompt isn't.

    For each criterion give the question, what a pass looks like, and what a fail looks like. The fail examples do most of the work; they're what stop the judge grading on general impression.

  2. Constrain the judge's output to a schema.

    Why: you learned this in sub-task 14 and it applies here for the same reasons. Per-criterion scores plus a short justification for anything below full marks. A judge that returns prose is a judge whose output you have to read one at a time.

    the judge's output shape 
    {
  "account_id": "...",
  "evidence_grounding":     {"score": 0-2, "note": "..."},
  "citation_validity":      {"score": 0-2, "note": "..."},
  "citation_relevance":     {"score": 0-2, "note": "..."},
  "reasoning_validity":     {"score": 0-2, "note": "..."},
  "severity_justification": {"score": 0-2, "note": "..."},
  "exemption_consideration":{"score": 0-2, "note": "..."},
  "worst_problem": "..."
}

    A three-point scale — fails, partial, passes — is easier to apply consistently than five or ten. Narrow scales are less expressive and much more reproducible, and reproducibility is the point.

  3. Give the judge only what it needs.

    Why: the finding, the rubric, and the list of ten legitimate citation identifiers. Not the dossier, not the policies in full, and definitely not your micro-key — you're assessing whether the finding stands on its own, which is exactly the position an auditor is in.

Stuck?

It gives everything full marks. The rubric is too vague, or the criteria are too easy to satisfy. Add the fail examples. Then test it deliberately: feed it a finding you've damaged — delete an evidence entry, swap in ACP-4.2 on a dormancy case — and check it catches the specific thing you broke.

It's inconsistent across runs. Same problem as sub-task 30: where the rubric is silent, the judge improvises. Run the same finding three times and tighten whichever criterion moves.

Should the judge use a different model? A reasonable instinct, and worth testing rather than assuming. What actually matters more is that it sees a different prompt and a different framing — it's evaluating an artifact, not continuing a reasoning process.

Task 2

Score everything, then read the worst

  1. Score all nine findings and every escalation.

    Why: escalations need judging too, and against slightly different criteria — does it state what's missing, and is "what would resolve it" something a reviewer could actually obtain? An escalation that says "unclear" scores badly for the same reason a finding with no citation does.

  2. Read the three worst yourself.

    Why: the sub-task's real work, and the only way to find out whether your judge is any good. For each, decide whether the judge was right. Sometimes it will have caught something real. Sometimes it will have penalised a perfectly good finding because your rubric expects something the case didn't warrant — and that's a rubric bug worth fixing.

    Look for these three patterns specifically, because they're the ones that recur. A rationale asserting a fact that isn't in the evidence array. A citation that's real but governs a different control. A severity stated and never argued.

  3. Check the judge against the micro-key.

    Why: the sharpest calibration available, and it produces a genuinely interesting result. A finding you labelled correct that scores badly on defensibility is a right answer badly argued — which will still fail with an auditor, and is worth fixing. A finding you labelled wrong that scores well is a wrong answer argued convincingly, which is the more dangerous failure and exactly what a judge cannot catch.

    That second case is the honest limit of this technique, and you should be able to state it. Defensibility and correctness are different axes. A system can score well on one and badly on the other, and only the micro-key sees the difference.

  4. Fix one thing the judge found, and re-score.

    Why: close the loop. The commonest fix is a Skill that says what a rationale must contain — every factual claim traceable to evidence, severity argued rather than stated, relevant exemptions addressed even when ruled out. Re-run and confirm the scores moved.

  5. Write up what the judge is worth.

  6. Commit the rubric and the scorer.

    The rubric is the specification of a good finding. It belongs in the repo more than most of the code does.

Stuck?

Isn't this circular? Partly, and it's worth being precise about which part. A judge cannot verify facts it has no access to — that's circular. It can check whether a rationale's claims appear in its own evidence array, whether a citation is on a list of ten strings, and whether a conclusion follows from stated premises. Those are textual properties of the artifact and there's nothing circular about assessing them.

The judge costs as much as the pipeline. Possible — it's one call per finding, and findings are the small end of the workload. If it matters, score a sample rather than everything, or score only findings that a cheaper check has already flagged as suspicious.

Can I use the judge to pick between two prompts? Yes, and that's one of its best uses. Run both, judge both, compare score distributions. Just don't optimise against the judge alone — you'll get findings that score well and say nothing, which is what the micro-key is there to catch.

You're done when
  • A rubric file with per-criterion questions, pass conditions and fail examples
  • Per-finding defensibility scores across every finding and escalation
  • The worst three read by hand, with a verdict on whether the judge was right
  • Judge scores cross-checked against the micro-key, and the blind spot stated
  • One fix made and its effect measured

All five effectiveness evaluations are built. You can detect a broken run, an unstable one, one that disagrees with a careful human, one that is overconfident, and one that argues badly.
