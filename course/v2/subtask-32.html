Decide when to escalate to a human

Depends on: Sub-task 31 — the cases you found hard are the ones this is for Time: ≈ 60 min Type: evals · human in the loop
First appearance
confidence threshold tri-state output review queue escalation rate

The system stops pretending to be certain. "I don't know" becomes a supported answer.

Your pipeline currently has two answers: this is a finding, or it isn't. Every account gets one, whether or not the evidence supports it.

You already know that's wrong, because sub-task 31 made you label twelve accounts and at least one of them you weren't sure about. You had a third option available — write "uncertain" and say what would resolve it. Your system doesn't.

This sub-task gives it one, and then measures how often it uses it — because a third option that fires on 60% of accounts is not a safety feature, it's an abdication.

What you'll understand and do

  • Explain why a forced binary is worse than an honest third state
  • Add a needs_human_review path without breaking the frozen contract
  • Set a threshold on something more defensible than a confidence number
  • Measure the escalation rate and justify it

Concept

The one idea

A system that can't say "I don't know" will say something else instead.

Force a binary and every uncertain case gets pushed to one side. Which side depends on your prompt's tone, and both directions are bad in ways that compound.

Push toward flagging and you get false positives. Reviewers spend time clearing accounts that were fine, and after a few quarters they start clearing your findings by reflex — which destroys the value of the true ones. Push toward clearing and you get false negatives: invisible, unmeasurable, and exactly what sub-task 18's contractor trap produced.

Tri-state output

Finding — the evidence supports it; here's the clause.

Clear — the evidence rules it out; here's why.

Needs human review — the evidence is genuinely insufficient. Here is what I found, what's missing, and what would resolve it.

The third state is not a failure mode. It's a correct answer to a question the available evidence doesn't settle, and a reviewer who receives it can act on it in a way they cannot act on a hedged finding.

Confidence numbers are a weak threshold, and it's worth knowing why before you build on one. A model's stated confidence is a token it produced, not a calibrated probability. A finding at 0.7 is not reliably twice as uncertain as one at 0.35. You can use it as one input, but a threshold built on it alone will be arbitrary.

Better escalation triggers — and where each comes from

Missing evidence. A dormancy judgment with no last_login isn't low-confidence, it's unfounded. The absence is a fact you can test for in code.

Unmatched identity. Sub-task 9 left accounts genuinely ambiguous — two employees fit the same slug. Adjudicating access for a person you cannot name is not something to do at 0.6 confidence.

Conflicting signals. An exemption's conditions partly met. A ticket that looks like a renewal and isn't. A001115 is the shape.

Novelty. A role pair or category the Skills don't cover. Silence in the specification should escalate, not default.

Every one of these is a structural test, not a vibe — which means you can implement it, test it, and explain it.

The contract constrains where this can live. recommendation is an enum of revoke / review / retain, and additionalProperties is false, so you cannot add a field. Two honest options: use review as the escalation signal with the rationale saying what's missing, or keep escalations out of findings.json entirely and write a separate queue file. The second is cleaner — an escalation isn't a finding, and putting it in the findings file inflates your count with things you haven't concluded.

Do this

Task 1

Add the third state

  1. Decide where escalations live, and write down why.

    Why: this is a contract decision and it has consequences. A separate review_queue.json keeps findings.json meaning "things I concluded", which is what a client's auditor expects it to mean. Using recommendation: "review" keeps everything in one file at the cost of conflating "I think this needs a look" with "I couldn't decide".

  2. Define your escalation triggers as testable conditions.

    Why: implement at least three, and make each one something code or a Skill can check rather than something a model feels. Missing evidence is the easiest and most valuable: if the judgment depends on a field and the field is absent, escalate rather than infer.

    an escalation record 
    {
  "account_id": "...",
  "reason": "ambiguous_identity",
  "what_i_found": "...",
  "what_is_missing": "two employees match this account name; cannot
                      determine employment status or term date",
  "what_would_resolve_it": "confirmation from HR of which employee
                            owns this account",
  "provisional_view": "would be a contractor overstay if E01143"
}

    The last three fields are what make this useful rather than an admission of defeat. A reviewer can act on "here's what I'd need". They cannot act on "uncertain".

  3. Teach the Skills that escalation is a legitimate output.

    Why: same lesson as sub-task 17's exemptions. If the Skills only describe findings and clearances, the model has no vocabulary for the third state and will force a binary regardless of what your code supports. Say explicitly when to escalate and what an escalation must contain.

Stuck?

Nothing escalates. Your triggers are too narrow, or genuinely nothing in the sample qualifies. Test it deliberately: strip last_login from one dossier and confirm the account escalates rather than being confidently cleared. If it gets cleared, that's the finding.

Everything escalates. Too broad. The commonest cause is treating low model confidence as a trigger on its own, which catches every marginal case in the quarter. Structural triggers are narrower and more defensible.

Doesn't this just move work back to humans? Some, deliberately, and the framing matters: the value isn't that the system decided everything, it's that it decided the 87% that were decidable and told you precisely why the rest weren't. That's a better product than a system that guesses on all of it.

Task 2

Measure the rate and defend it

  1. Run the quarter and count what landed in the queue.

    Why: the escalation rate is a headline number and you'll be asked for it. Express it against the candidate set — N of 166 accounts reaching the agent layer — not against all 1,200, since the other 1,034 were settled deterministically and were never candidates for escalation.

  2. Read every escalation and check it deserved to be one.

    Why: this is the calibration step. For each, ask whether you could have decided it from the dossier. If you could, the system escalated something it should have handled and the trigger is too broad. If you couldn't, it's correct — and the "what would resolve it" field should be something a reviewer can actually get.

  3. Score the queue against your micro-key.

    Why: the sharpest test available. An account you labelled with high confidence that the system escalated is a false escalation. An account you found genuinely hard that it escalated is a calibrated one. And an account you found hard that it decided confidently is the most interesting result of all — overconfidence exactly where you hesitated.

  4. Write the number down with its justification.
    My escalation policy

    This is a client-facing number. Write it as one.

    Saved in this browser
  5. Check the validator still passes, and commit.

    Why: findings.json may now contain fewer findings, since some accounts moved to the queue. Your sub-task 29 coverage checks should still pass, and if a "not empty" check now fails, that's the validator doing its job on a real change.

Stuck?

What's a good escalation rate? There isn't a universal number, and anyone who gives you one is guessing. What matters is that it's justified: every escalation is a case you agree is undecidable, and every decided case is one you agree was decidable. A defensible 15% beats an arbitrary 5%.

The nine findings dropped to seven. Investigate before accepting. Which two moved, and were they genuinely undecidable? A000686 moving to the queue would be the trigger firing on "no ticket exists" — but absence of a ticket is exactly the evidence the standard asks for, so that's a trigger that needs narrowing.

Should escalated accounts count as findings for the client? No, and the distinction is worth stating explicitly in the report. Findings are conclusions. Escalations are open questions. Presenting them together inflates the finding count with work you haven't done.

You're done when
  • A third output state exists, with a deliberate decision about where it lives
  • Three or more structural escalation triggers — not confidence numbers alone
  • Escalation records carrying what's missing and what would resolve it
  • A measured rate against the candidate set, with every escalation checked by hand
  • Calibration scored against the micro-key

Your system now declines to answer questions it can't answer, and you can say how often that happens and why.

What you still can't say is whether the findings it did produce are well-argued. Sub-task 33 grades the reasoning rather than the answer.

If you're curious

  • Take three escalations to a colleague and ask them to decide. Where they can and you couldn't, the trigger is too broad. Where they also can't, you've found a genuine limitation of the evidence — which is a finding about the client's data quality and worth reporting as one.
  • Calibration — the statistical framing of what it means for a stated confidence to be trustworthy, and why an uncalibrated number is a bad threshold.
