Hand-label twelve dossiers and score your system against yourself

Depends on: Sub-task 30 — you need a stable pipeline before measuring it against a target Time: ≈ 90 min Type: evals · ground truth you make
First appearance
micro-key regression suite cold labelling

The only ground truth you are allowed is the ground truth you make. This is where you make it.

Everything so far has measured your system against itself. Schema conformance, distribution plausibility, run-to-run agreement — all internal. A system can pass every one of them and be confidently, consistently wrong.

The fix is unglamorous. Sit down with a dozen dossiers, read them cold, write your own verdict on each, and only then look at what the pipeline said. Two or three hours, no automation, no shortcut.

What you get is small, expensive, and the most valuable artifact in Theme H — because it's the only one that can tell you the system is wrong in a direction you didn't anticipate.

What you'll understand and do

  • Select twelve to fifteen dossiers that cover the interesting cases
  • Label them cold, before seeing any pipeline output
  • Score the pipeline against your labels and read every disagreement
  • Freeze the result as a regression test

Concept

The one idea

A dozen cases you labelled yourself beats a thousand you didn't.

You cannot label 1,200 accounts. You can label twelve properly — reading the dossier carefully, checking the policy, forming a view you'd defend. Twelve careful labels are worth more than a hundred careless ones, because the value is entirely in the care.

Cold labelling, and why the order is non-negotiable

Label before you look at the pipeline's answer. Every time.

Read the pipeline's verdict first and you cannot un-read it. It becomes the anchor you reason from, and you will find yourself constructing a justification for it rather than an independent view. This is not a discipline failing; it is how anchoring works, and the only defense is procedural.

You already did one of these, in sub-task 13, when you wrote your own verdict on A000012 before comparing. Same principle, eleven more times.

Selection matters as much as labelling. Twelve random accounts from 1,200 will be twelve accounts with nothing wrong, and a key of twelve clean accounts measures almost nothing.

What the twelve should cover

Known findings — at least one from each category, so you can detect a whole category silently breaking.

Known exemptions — an ACP-4.2 Controller and a break-glass account. False positives on these are the ones that lose the room.

Near-misses — A001115 is the canonical one: right department, wrong title. Anything sitting near a threshold.

Genuinely clean accounts — several. 87% of Meridian's accounts are provably clean, and a key with no clean accounts cannot detect a system that flags everything.

At least one you find genuinely hard. If your own label is uncertain, say so in the key. That uncertainty is data, and sub-task 32 is about what a system should do with cases like it.

And then it becomes a regression test. That's the second life of this artifact and arguably the bigger one. Every prompt change, Skill edit and model swap from here on can be scored against the same twelve in seconds. Sub-task 30 told you the system agrees with itself; the micro-key tells you whether a change moved it toward or away from a fixed target.

Do this

Task 1

Choose, then label cold

  1. Pick twelve to fifteen accounts against the coverage list.

    Why: write down why each one is in the set before you start. "Covers the contractor category", "near-miss on the ACP-4.2 exemption", "should be entirely clean". A set whose composition you can justify is one you can defend and extend.

    a defensible twelve 
    findings      A000384  SoD + terminated       A001115  SoD near-miss
              A000012  dormant Vault Admin    A000598  dormant Vault Admin
              A001185  contractor overstay    A000686  contractor twin account
exemptions    A000387  ACP-4.2 Controller     A001159  break-glass
clean         four ordinary accounts with nothing wrong
hard          one you genuinely aren't sure about

    A000686 earns its place: it's Jamie Alvarez's second account, with no ticket of its own, and it is the single easiest finding in the quarter to miss. A key that contains it will catch that regression forever.

  2. Generate the dossiers and put the pipeline output out of reach.

    Why: literally. Move findings.json aside, or work in a separate file. Make it inconvenient to peek, because you will otherwise peek.

  3. Label each one, with a rationale you'd defend.

    Why: for each account record whether it's a finding, which category, what severity, what recommendation, and — the important part — why, citing the clause. Your reasoning is what makes a disagreement adjudicable later.

    micro_key.json — one entry per account 
    {
  "account_id": "A001115",
  "is_finding": true,
  "category": "sod_conflict",
  "severity": "critical",
  "recommendation": "revoke",
  "policy": "ACP-4.1",
  "why": "Holds Vendor Admin + AP Manager in Atlas ERP. In Finance &
          Accounting but title is Senior Accountant, not Controller, so
          ACP-4.2 does not reach them.",
  "my_confidence": "high",
  "labelled_before_seeing_output": true
}

    Keep my_confidence honest. A case where you hesitated is more informative than one you were sure about — it marks the boundary of what the specification can reasonably be expected to settle.

Stuck?

I already know the answers from Theme D. Partly, and that's fine — the accounts are known, but the severity, recommendation and confidence are still yours to decide, and those are where disagreement lives. Include accounts this course never told you about; the clean ones and the near-misses are the ones you're genuinely labelling.

Twelve feels too few. It is few, and it's the right number for the time available. The alternative isn't a hundred careful labels, it's a hundred careless ones, which is worse than twelve careful ones in a way that isn't obvious until you try it.

I can't decide on one. Record that. "my_confidence": "low" with a note on what would resolve it is a legitimate entry, and it feeds directly into sub-task 32.

Task 2

Score, then read every disagreement

  1. Score the pipeline against the key.

    Why: on a set this small, report counts rather than percentages — "agreed on 10 of 12" is honest, "83.3% accuracy" implies a precision the sample size doesn't support. Break it down by what disagreed: finding-vs-no-finding, category, severity, recommendation.

    the scoreboard 
    agreed on is_finding      N of 12
  false positives         flagged, I said clean
  false negatives         I flagged, it said clean
agreed on category        N of the findings we both found
agreed on severity        N
agreed on recommendation  N
  2. Read every disagreement and decide who was right.

    Why: this is where the sub-task earns its ninety minutes, and the honest answer is sometimes that the pipeline was right and you weren't. That happens most often when it found a fact you skimmed past — and when it does, the key gets corrected, with a note saying why.

    Three outcomes, and each leads somewhere different. The pipeline was wrong → a Skill or prompt needs fixing. You were wrong → correct the key and note the correction. Both defensible → the policy is genuinely ambiguous, which is the most interesting outcome and belongs in a report to the client, not in a code change.

  3. Freeze it as a regression test.

    Why: make it runnable in one command against the twelve accounts, and add it to your test suite. From here on, every change to a prompt, a Skill or a model gets scored against it before you believe it improved anything.

    Set the pass bar deliberately. Exact agreement on all twelve is probably too strict — a severity moving one step isn't a regression. Agreement on every is_finding is the right hard requirement, with graded fields reported rather than enforced.

  4. Write up what the key says about your system.
    My micro-key results

    Sub-tasks 35, 36 and 39 all score against this. It is your fixed point.

    Saved in this browser
  5. Commit the key and the scorer.

    micro_key.json is source, not output. It represents hours of judgment and it should be reviewable, versioned, and arguable.

Stuck?

It agreed on all twelve. Then your set is too easy. Add the boundary cases — an account near the 180-day line, a Finance employee whose title is adjacent to Controller, a contractor whose term date is just after quarter end. A key that always passes tells you nothing.

It disagreed on most of them. Check for a systematic cause before concluding the system is bad — an as-of date mismatch or a Skill that didn't load will produce broad disagreement from a single root cause.

Isn't my judgment also fallible? Yes, and this is the honest limit of the technique. What a micro-key measures is agreement with a careful human reviewer, not truth. On a real engagement you'd have the client's own reviewer label a sample, which measures agreement with the person whose opinion actually governs.

You're done when
  • micro_key.json with 12–15 hand-labelled accounts, each with a defended rationale
  • Every label made cold, before seeing pipeline output
  • A score broken down by is_finding, category, severity and recommendation
  • Every disagreement adjudicated, with the key corrected where you were wrong
  • A one-command regression test with a deliberately chosen pass bar

You have ground truth. It's small and it's yours, and it will catch every regression from here to the end of the course.

One thing it surfaced: cases where you couldn't decide. Sub-task 32 asks what a system should do when it can't either.

If you're curious

  • Have someone else label three of your twelve without seeing your key. Where you disagree with them, the policy is ambiguous rather than the system being wrong — and inter-rater disagreement is the cleanest possible evidence for a clause that needs clarifying.
  • Score a deliberately damaged pipeline against the key — drop a Skill and re-run. Watching the score fall is how you learn what the key is sensitive to, and what it would miss.
