Put a dollar figure and a clock on one quarterly review

Depends on: Sub-task 33 — you need the full pipeline including judging Time: ≈ 45 min Type: evals · efficiency
First appearance
cost per finding latency SLA

The first efficiency eval, and the numbers that decide whether there is a phase two.

Everything so far has answered "is it any good?". The person approving the budget asks a different question, and asks it first.

What does one quarterly review cost, and how long does it take? Not roughly. A number, with the method behind it, that survives being challenged.

You have been logging usage since sub-task 14. This is where it becomes a figure you can put on a slide.

What you'll understand and do

  • Aggregate token usage across every call in a complete run
  • Compute cost per review, cost per finding, and cost per candidate
  • Measure wall clock and understand what dominates it
  • State the numbers with their assumptions attached

Concept

The one idea

Three numbers, and the third is the one that gets quoted.

Cost per review is the total for a quarter. It's the number that goes in a budget line and it's the easiest to compute.

Cost per candidate is the total divided by the 166 accounts that reached the agent layer. It's the number that tells you what happens if the client's next engagement has 12,000 accounts instead of 1,200, and it's the one you should reason with internally.

Cost per finding is the total divided by nine. It is the number that gets quoted back at you, and it is dangerously misleading — which is exactly why you need to compute it yourself and be ready to explain it.

Why cost per finding is a trap

A quarter with few findings has a high cost per finding. That reads as poor value and it is precisely backwards: a clean quarter is a good outcome. You did the same work to establish that 1,034 accounts were fine, and establishing that is most of what an access review is for.

Compute it anyway, because someone will. Then say the other sentence in the same breath: the cost is per account reviewed, not per problem found, and a quarter with no findings costs the same and is worth just as much.

Count everything. The judge calls from sub-task 33 are part of the cost. So are retries, failed calls, the tool-use loop's repeated resends, and every call your subagents made. A figure that counts only the successful judgment calls is one that will be wrong the first time you scale, and wrong in the expensive direction.

Latency, and what it's for

Wall clock for a full limit=None run. This is not a user-facing latency — nobody is waiting on a quarterly review — but it constrains the iteration loop and it answers "can this run overnight in the maintenance window?"

Note what dominates. If it's serialized model calls, concurrency helps. If it's rate limits, more concurrency makes it worse. Knowing which is which is what stops you optimising the wrong thing in sub-task 36.

Do this

Task 1

Aggregate

  1. Make sure every call is logged, including the ones you forget.

    Why: audit your instrumentation before you trust its output. Each subagent's calls, every iteration of every tool-use loop, the judge, and any retries. A run that made 400 calls and logged 180 gives you a confident number that's less than half the real one.

    Sanity check: multiply your logged call count by a plausible average and see whether it lands near the total. Large discrepancies mean something isn't logging.

  2. Run the full quarter and time it.

    Why: one clean limit=None run with complete instrumentation. Record wall clock, total input tokens, total output tokens, and the call count, broken down by which component made them.

    terminal 
    time python -m meridian_capstone.contract.run_review data/2026-Q3 > findings.json
  3. Convert to dollars using current published rates.

    Why: input and output are priced differently and output is several times more expensive, so a run that's output-heavy costs more than the token count suggests. If you used more than one model, price each separately — that breakdown is what makes sub-task 35's argument concrete.

    Look the rates up rather than trusting a number from a course page. They change, and a stale figure in a client deck is worse than no figure.

Task 2

State the numbers, with their assumptions

  1. Compute all three, with the right denominators.

    Why: total cost. Divided by 166 candidates. Divided by 9 findings. And note the fourth figure someone will compute themselves — divided by 1,200 total accounts — which looks flatteringly small and is the least honest of the four, since 1,034 of those accounts never reached a model at all.

    the four denominators 
    per quarter      total
per candidate    total / 166      accounts reaching the agent layer
per finding      total / 9        the quotable one, and the misleading one
per account      total / 1200     flattering; 1,034 never saw a model
  2. Break the cost down by component.

    Why: this is where the optimisation targets are. Which of ledger-reader, policy-applier, findings-writer and the judge dominates? Almost always the tool-use loop in ledger-reader, because every iteration resends everything before it. That single observation is what makes sub-tasks 35 and 36 obvious rather than speculative.

  3. Say what dominates the wall clock.

    Why: serialized calls, rate limiting, or a slow local step are three different problems with three different fixes, and the wrong diagnosis wastes sub-task 36. If you saw 429s during the full run, that's the answer.

  4. Write the sentence you'd say out loud.

    Why: the deliverable is one sentence a manager can repeat, plus the caveats that stop it being misused.

    The cost of a quarter

    Sub-tasks 35 and 36 both try to move these numbers. This is the baseline — record it precisely.

    Saved in this browser
  5. Compare against your sub-task 14 projection, and commit.

    Why: you estimated this from one call, before you had a pipeline. How far off were you, and in which direction? Almost certainly low, because the estimate assumed one call per account and the reality is a tool-use loop across three agents plus a judge. Knowing the size of that gap is what makes your next estimate better.

Stuck?

My cost is much higher than I expected. Look at the tool-use loop first. Every iteration resends the whole conversation, so an agent making six tool calls per account is paying for that conversation seven times, growing each time. That single mechanism dominates most agentic cost bills.

Should I include my own debugging runs? Not in the per-quarter figure — that's the cost of a production run. But track development cost separately and be ready to state it, because on a real engagement someone will ask what it cost to build, and "I don't know" is a bad answer.

The numbers feel too small to matter. For 1,200 accounts, quite possibly. Multiply by a realistic enterprise — tens of thousands of accounts, quarterly, across several regulated entities — and it stops being negligible. That multiplication is the argument for sub-tasks 35 and 36.

You're done when
  • Every model call in a full run logged, with the instrumentation cross-checked
  • Cost per quarter, per candidate, per finding and per account — with the right denominator identified for each
  • Cost attributed by component, so you know what to optimise
  • Wall clock measured and its bottleneck diagnosed
  • One quotable sentence, with the caveats that stop it being misused

You can now answer the budget question. The next two sub-tasks try to make the answer smaller.

If you're curious

  • Token counting — count_tokens lets you price a request before making it, which is how you estimate a client's bill without running their whole quarter.
  • Work out the break-even against a human reviewer. At a plausible hourly rate, how many accounts per hour would a person need to clear to match your cost per candidate? The answer is usually the most persuasive number in the whole deck.
