Name the access-review headache you'll test everything against

Depends on: nothing — start here Time: ≈ 30 min Type: orientation — no code
First appearance
agentic system the six building blocks Meridian Regional Energy

Three ideas you'll use in every remaining sub-task. Each one is explained in full below — you don't need to know any of them yet.

This course has a shared practice case: a fictional utility called Meridian Regional Energy with a genuinely messy access problem. But a practice case only ever teaches you the practice case. So before you touch a single tool, you're going to write down your own access-review headache — the real one, from your real job — and carry it alongside Meridian's the whole way through.

That note is the quietest, most important artifact you'll make. Every checkpoint from here on asks the same question: could what you just learned have moved your problem? Without a written problem, that question has no teeth — it's very easy to follow thirty-eight sub-tasks, get everything green, and still not know whether any of it applies to Monday.

What you'll understand and do

  • Explain what makes a system agentic rather than merely automated — and why that distinction changes what you can hand it
  • Name the six building blocks this course assembles, and what each one is for
  • State one access-review problem from your own work in terms specific enough to test a system against

Concept

The one idea

A practice case teaches the practice. Your own case teaches you.

Everything in this course is built on a fixed data set from a fictional utility. That's deliberate — a frozen, shared case means the numbers are checkable, and when something you build is wrong, you can prove it's wrong instead of arguing about it. You need that to learn.

But a frozen case has a failure mode, and it's the one that quietly ruins most technical training: you get good at the exercise. The exercise ends. Nothing transfers. The fix is cheap and takes thirty minutes: name a real problem of your own now, in writing, before you know any of the answers — so it can't be retro-fitted to whatever you happen to end up building.

What is an "agentic system"?

A regular automation follows steps you wrote: read this column, compare it to that list, flag anything over 90 days. It does exactly that, forever. If the input shifts, it breaks or — worse — silently returns nonsense.

An agentic system is given an outcome and some judgment, not a fixed sequence: here is the access data, here is our policy, find the violations and show me your evidence. It decides which lookups to make, in what order, and when it has enough to answer. That's the trade: it can handle the mess and ambiguity a rule can't, and in exchange it can be confidently wrong in ways a rule never is. Most of this course is about the second half of that sentence — how you constrain, verify, and audit something that exercises judgment.

Who is Meridian Regional Energy?

A fictional mid-size utility, and your practice case. A regulator has asked Meridian a question that ought to be easy — show us everyone who can approve a vendor payment — and nobody can answer it. Not because the access doesn't exist, but because it's scattered across an HR system, an entitlements export, a ticketing queue, and five policy documents that have never spoken to each other.

Meridian's data ships with this course and is intentionally messy: mismatched names, two date formats in one column, duplicate rows. That mess is the point. Clean data would teach you nothing about the job.

The six building blocks. The rest of the course assembles exactly six things. You're not expected to understand them yet — you're copying them down as a map, so that when each one arrives you already know where it sits and what it's for.

1
Rules

Standing instructions that apply to everything Claude does in your project — the things you'd otherwise repeat in every single prompt.

You build this in sub-task 7.

2
Skills

Written expertise Claude loads when it's relevant — here, Meridian's policy documents turned into checks Claude can actually apply.

Later in the course, once you have data to apply them to.

3
Tools

Live lookups into real systems — asking "who is this person, and is this account theirs?" at the moment the question comes up, instead of guessing from a stale export.

Later in the course.

4
Subagents

Splitting the work across several narrow specialists rather than asking one generalist to hold the entire review in its head at once.

Later in the course.

5
Hooks

Automatic checks that fire around the system's actions — one to block what shouldn't happen, one to record what did. This is your audit trail.

Later in the course.

6
Generalization

Proving the thing you built works on data it has never seen — a different quarter, different people, different violations. The difference between a system and a very elaborate answer key.

The final theme — and the real test.

Six blocks, and the last one is the only one that matters to your boss. Keep that in view.

Task 1

Write down your own access-review headache

  1. Pick one access-review question from your own work that is genuinely annoying to answer today.

    Why: "genuinely annoying" is the filter. If it's already easy, there's nothing for a system to do. If it's so vague you can't tell whether an answer is right, you won't be able to test anything against it.

  2. Fill in the four fields below.

    Why: these four turn a vague complaint ("access reviews are painful") into something checkable ("I can't tell which of our 40 contractors still have Salesforce admin, and it takes me two days of spreadsheet work to find out"). Your notes save in this browser automatically.

    My headache

    Plain language. No jargon, no tooling — you're describing the problem, not the solution. Two or three sentences per box is plenty.

    Saved in this browser
  3. Save it somewhere outside this page too.

    Why: this page keeps your text in your browser's local storage, which is convenient but not durable — clearing site data wipes it. Hit Copy my note and paste it into whatever you actually keep notes in. You'll be asked to reread it many times.

Stuck?

"I don't run access reviews — I'm here for the agent part." Fine. Use any recurring question at your job where the answer is scattered across systems that don't share an identifier, and where being wrong has consequences. Vendor risk, contract renewals, data retention, incident review — the shape is what matters, not the subject.

"My real problem is too big for this." Good — most real ones are. Don't shrink it. Write the big version, then add one line at the end: "the smallest useful slice of this is ___". Later sub-tasks will keep asking you to name that slice, and it's much easier to cut down from the true problem than to build up from a toy one.

"I can't say what systems it lives in — that's confidential." Describe them generically: "the HR system", "the identity provider", "a shared spreadsheet owned by one person in Finance". The structure is what you need. Never paste real employee names, account IDs, or client data into this page or into any prompt in this course.

"I filled it in but it feels thin." Check field 4. If you couldn't write concrete manual steps and a real elapsed time, the problem is still too abstract — you're describing a category, not a task. Push until you can say what you'd physically do on a Tuesday morning.

Task 2

Copy the six blocks down, with a guess against each

  1. For each of the six blocks, write one line: how might it apply to your headache?

    Why: guessing badly right now is the entire point. These guesses get compared against reality as each block arrives, and being wrong in a specific, written way is what makes the correction stick. A vague guess can't be wrong, so it can't teach you anything.

    Six blocks vs. my headache

    One line each. "No idea yet" is an acceptable answer for any of them — write that rather than leaving it blank, so you can tell later which ones genuinely surprised you.

    Saved in this browser
Stuck?

"I can't guess — I don't know what these words mean." Expected, and fine. Read only the one-line description in the table above and guess from that alone. You are not being graded; you're planting a flag so you can see how far you moved.

"Several of them sound like the same thing." They do at this stage — especially Rules vs. Skills, and Tools vs. Subagents. Write down which pairs feel blurry to you. Those exact confusions get resolved directly when the blocks arrive, and noticing them now makes that moment land harder.

You're done when

You have a one-page note — in your own notes app, not just in this browser — containing both halves:

  • Your access-review headache, with the question, the asker, the systems, and the current manual cost
  • All six building blocks, each with one line guessing how it might apply

That page is the reference point for every checkpoint that follows. When a later sub-task says "check this against your note," this is the note.
