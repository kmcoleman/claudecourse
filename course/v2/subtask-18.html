Build two more Skills and fall into the contractor trap

Depends on: Sub-task 17 — you need a working Skill as the pattern Time: ≈ 90 min Type: skills · policy ambiguity
First appearance
policy ambiguity false-negative trap

The most important sub-task in Theme D. A plausible reading of one sentence silently clears four real violations.

Two more Skills. The Privileged Access Standard is straightforward work with one scope limit that matters. The Contractor Access Standard is the one this sub-task is really about.

Its operative sentence is twenty-three words long. There is a reading of it that any competent reviewer would call reasonable, that produces a clean report, and that is wrong in a way nothing in your pipeline will tell you about. Four real findings vanish. Every test still passes. The report looks better.

You're going to fall into it deliberately, watch the findings disappear, and then work out what the sentence actually requires.

What you'll understand and do

  • Author the Privileged Access Standard Skill, both requirements and the scoped carve-out
  • Author the Contractor Access Standard Skill and get the wrong answer on purpose
  • Locate the exact word in the clause that the plausible reading drops
  • Write down the general lesson, not just the fix

Concept

The one idea

A false negative is invisible. That is the entire problem with it.

A false positive announces itself. Someone gets flagged, they push back, you look again, you apologise. Painful, self-correcting, over in a day.

A false negative produces a shorter report and a quieter week. Nothing in the pipeline knows. No test fails, because you'd have to already know the right answer to write the test. Your evaluation suite says everything is green, because green is exactly what a missed finding looks like from the inside.

Where these come from

Not from a model being wrong. From a defensible reading of an ambiguous sentence that happens not to be the one the author meant.

You will not catch this by testing harder. You catch it by reading the clause adversarially — asking of each phrase "if I drop this word, what stops being a finding?" — and by having ground truth to check against.

Meridian's Contractor Access Standard says, in full:

Contractor access ends on the statement-of-work end date. Extensions require a new approved ticket referencing the renewed SOW before the prior end date.

The plausible reading is: an approved ticket dated before the end date means the access was extended, so it's fine. That reading is not lazy. It is the reading most people produce on a first pass, because it's how extension processes usually work.

Now look at the data it meets. Three of Meridian's expired contractors have exactly that — an approved ticket dated a few days before their term date. Under the plausible reading, all three are cleared. And the tickets grant VPN / User and reference no statement of work at all, because no ticket in the entire dataset has an SOW field of any kind.

So under the clause as written, no ticket in this dataset can extend anything. Under the plausible reading, three do. That gap is four findings wide.

The second trap, in the same document

All 117 contractors have status: active in the HR roster. A rule keyed on employment status finds nothing at all — not fewer findings, zero. Only term_date reveals the lapse, and a contractor whose SOW ended is still "active" until someone deprovisions them, which is precisely the failure the control exists to catch.

Do this

Task 1

The Privileged Access Standard Skill

  1. Author .claude/skills/privileged-access-standard/SKILL.md with both requirements.

    Why: the standard sets two independent conditions, and people routinely encode one. An approved access request before the grant, and activity within 180 days. An account can breach either, or both, and the rationale should say which.

    Cite by title. This document has no clause numbers — a "§2.1" here is a fabrication, and your Skill should say so in as many words.

  2. Get the break-glass carve-out's scope exactly right.

    Why: the sentence is "Break-glass accounts are exempt from the dormancy rule when registered and documented." Two limits, both load-bearing. It exempts from the dormancy rule only — a break-glass account with no approving ticket is still a finding on that half. And it applies only when registered, which means present in data/2026-Q3/service_accounts.json. A Skill that reads "break-glass accounts are exempt" full stop over-applies on both axes.

  3. Test it and check the numbers.

    Why: there are 105 privileged grants in the ledger, of which 23 carry no approving ticket, and Vault alone has 77 Admin grants. Out of all that, exactly three are stale enough to breach the 180-day rule, and all three also breach the ticket requirement.

    expected — dormant privileged 
    FINDING  A000012  gabriel.villa     Vault/Admin  last login 2025-11-14
FINDING  A000598  robert.miller     Vault/Admin  last login 2025-09-14
FINDING  A000742  debbie.gonzalez   Vault/Admin  last login 2025-10-27

EXEMPT   A001159  emergency.admin   Active Directory / Domain Admin
EXEMPT   A001199  emergency.admin   Active Directory / Domain Admin

    All three findings are Vault Admins, all granted 2025-05-18 by gateway.provisioning, none with an approving ticket. Against a quarter end of 2026-09-30 the 180-day cutoff falls on 2026-04-03, and every one of those logins is months on the wrong side of it. A000012 is the account you have been judging since sub-task 13 — you now have a policy-grounded verdict on it.

  4. Confirm the two exempt accounts are exempt for the stated reason.

    Why: A001159 and A001199 are both emergency.admin, both Domain Admin in Active Directory, both registered in service_accounts.json, and both hold approved tickets — so they clear the dormancy rule by exemption and the ticket rule on the merits. If your Skill exempted them without checking the registration, it got the right answer by luck and will clear an unregistered break-glass account next quarter.

Stuck?

I got more than three dormant findings. Probably counting non- privileged grants, or applying dormancy to accounts rather than to grants. The rule is about privileged roles specifically.

Fewer than three. Check your as-of date. If you're measuring dormancy against today rather than quarter end you may be shifting the window. All three of these are stale by such a margin that the exact date doesn't change the answer — which is convenient now and a hazard in sub-task 38, where a hardcoded date does change it.

emergency.admin looks like a person. It does, deliberately — along with marcus.pipeline. That's the same trap as sub-task 9: any rule keyed on an svc- prefix misclassifies both. Registration is checked against the file, not against the shape of the name.

Task 2

Write the contractor Skill the obvious way, and watch it fail

  1. Author .claude/skills/contractor-access-standard/SKILL.md encoding the plausible reading: contractor access ends at the SOW date unless an approved ticket predates it.

    Why: write it the way you would have written it anyway. The exercise only works if the wrong version is one you'd genuinely have shipped.

  2. Run it over the contractor population and count what you get.

    Why: there are 117 contractors. Four accounts should be findings. Under the plausible reading you will get one, or none.

    the four real overstays — three people, four accounts 
    A000686  jamie.alvarez        E00175  term 2026-09-15   no ticket
A000741  jamie.alvarez        E00175  term 2026-09-15   REQ-A000741
A001185  anita.richardson     E01143  term 2026-09-12   REQ-A001185
A001195  jennifer.gallagher   E01163  term 2026-09-15   REQ-A001195

every ticket:  app VPN, role User, status approved,
               requested a few days BEFORE the term date,
               and referencing no SOW — because no ticket
               in this dataset has an SOW field at all

    Note A000686 and A000741 are the same person. Jamie Alvarez (E00175, a Contractor in Marketing) holds two accounts, and both lapse with the same SOW. There is also a second, unrelated Jamie Alvarez in the roster — E00577, an FTE in operations — which is the ambiguous-slug case from sub-task 9 coming back to bite. Match on account, not on name.

  3. Find the word.

    Why: read the clause once more and ask which phrase your Skill dropped. "a new approved ticket referencing the renewed SOW before the prior end date." Not a ticket. Not an approved ticket. A ticket that references the renewed statement of work. Your Skill checked approval and date, and skipped the object of the sentence.

    Then check whether any ticket could satisfy it. Open data/2026-Q3/access_tickets.json and look at the fields: ticket_id, account_id, app, role, requested_date, approver, status. No SOW field exists. Under the clause as written, no ticket in this dataset can extend any contractor's access — which makes the correct answer four findings, not one.

  4. Correct the Skill, and re-run to four.

    Why: the fix is not "be stricter". It is to make the Skill test the condition the clause states, and to say what to do when the evidence needed to satisfy it doesn't exist in the source data. A ticket that cannot reference an SOW cannot demonstrate a renewal, and a Skill that says so is one a reviewer can act on.

    While you're there: make sure the Skill never keys on status. All 117 contractors are active. The signal is term_date.

  5. Write the lesson down as a general principle.

    Why: the specific fix is worth ten minutes. The habit is worth the rest of your career — and this is the story you tell a client about why their "clean" review might not be.

    The contractor trap, written up

    Keep both readings. The gap between them is the whole lesson.

    Saved in this browser
  6. Commit both Skills.

    Commit the corrected contractor Skill. Consider keeping the wrong version's reasoning in a comment or a note — the next person to read the Skill benefits from knowing which reading was rejected and why.

Stuck?

I only found 3 accounts. You missed A000686 — Jamie Alvarez's second account. It has no ticket of its own, so a Skill that starts from the ticket list never reaches it. Start from the contractor population and their term dates, then look for tickets; not the other way round.

I found more than 4. Check that you're only flagging contractors whose term_date has passed relative to quarter end, and that you're not counting each grant as a separate finding. Four accounts, three people.

Isn't rejecting every ticket too strict? It's what the clause says, and the more useful framing is what a reviewer should do about it. The right output is a finding plus the observation that the ticketing system cannot record an SOW reference, which makes the control unverifiable by design. That second half is a genuinely valuable thing to hand a client.

Which Jamie Alvarez is which? E00175 is the Contractor in Marketing with a term date. E00577 is an FTE in operations with none. Two people, one firstname.lastname slug — exactly the ambiguity sub-task 9 warned you not to resolve by name.

You're done when
  • All three Skills exist and load: sod-policy, privileged-access-standard, contractor-access-standard
  • 3 dormant privileged findings — A000012, A000598, A000742 — with A001159 and A001199 exempted as registered break-glass
  • 4 contractor overstay findings across A000686, A000741, A001185 and A001195
  • The trap documented: both readings, the dropped phrase, the cost, and the general rule

Totals for the quarter now stand at 9 findings (2 SoD + 3 dormant + 4 contractor) and 5 exemptions (3 Controllers + 2 break-glass).

Sub-task 19 closes Theme D by taking A000012 — the account you have been judging since sub-task 13 — back to the same agent, now with these Skills in reach.

If you're curious

  • Open data/2026-Q3/access_tickets.json and read the schema. Eighty-five tickets, seven fields, no SOW reference anywhere. A control that cannot be evidenced by the system meant to evidence it is a finding about the process, not about any individual — and it is the kind of observation clients remember.
  • Type I and type II errors — the statistical framing. A false negative is a type II error, and the reason it dominates this domain is that the cost of missing a live privileged account dwarfs the cost of an awkward conversation.
