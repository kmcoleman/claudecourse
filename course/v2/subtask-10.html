Normalize the mess and watch the duplicate count move

First appearance

Short sub-task, one idea, one number that moves. The number is the lesson.

In sub-task 8 you counted ten exact duplicate rows. That number is about to change, and the reason it changes is the most portable thing in this theme.

Ten felt like a small, tidy problem. It was small only because you were comparing raw strings, and raw strings disagree about things that are actually the same. Normalize first and the same comparison finds three times as many.

What you'll understand and do

  • State the rule: normalize before you compare, never after
  • Convert a two-format date column to one canonical form with zero losses
  • Show the duplicate count before and after, and explain the gap

Concept

The one idea

"Duplicate" is not a property of the data. It's a property of your comparison.

Here are two rows from entitlements.csv, both belonging to account A000323:

the same grant, twice 
A000323,jessica.schultz,Box,Member,2026-07-21,helpdesk,2026-07-08
A000323,jessica.schultz,Box,Member,07/21/2026,helpdesk,2026-07-08

Same account. Same app. Same role. Same day. Same approver. To drop_duplicates() on the raw text, these are two distinct grants, because 2026-07-21 and 07/21/2026 are different strings. To a human they are obviously one grant recorded twice.

That's a hidden duplicate — a duplicate your comparison function can't see. And hidden duplicates outnumber the visible kind here two to one.

Normalization, in one sentence

Convert every value in a column to a single canonical form before comparing, joining, sorting, or grouping. Dates to ISO. Text to trimmed-and-lowercased. Then, and only then, ask whether two things are equal.

Order of operations is not a style choice here — it changes the answer. You have three ways to end up, and the kit's tests name all three:

×
15,188 rows — you didn't de-duplicate at all

Every grant counted as many times as it was recorded.

×
15,178 rows — de-duplicated, then normalized

You caught the 10 exact duplicates and missed 20 format-twins. This is the plausible wrong answer, and it looks completely fine.

✓
15,158 rows — normalized, then de-duplicated

All 30 removed. Same two operations, opposite order, 20 rows of difference.

Twenty rows out of fifteen thousand sounds like rounding. It isn't: each one is a grant that would appear twice in a report a client signs, and "your numbers are wrong" is not a survivable sentence in an audit deliverable.

Do this

Task 1

Canonicalize granted_date and lose nothing

  1. Parse both formats into one ISO column, and assert that every row parsed.

    Why: the assert is the point. Silent parse failures become NaT, and NaT compares false against everything, so a broken date turns into a grant that never triggers any rule. Your script must fail loudly rather than quietly lose rows.

    The convention, decided in sub-task 8: slash dates are MM/DD/YYYY. Getting this backwards moves 7,650 grants by up to three months, in the direction that determines whether a grant predates a hire date.

    expected output 
    granted_date parsed:  15188 / 15188
unparsed (NaT):           0
distinct formats after:   1  (YYYY-MM-DD)
  2. Spot-check a slash date you can verify by hand.

    Why: "0 unparsed" only proves it parsed, not that it parsed correctly — 04/07/2025 parses cleanly under both conventions. Find a value where the day exceeds 12, such as 04/23/2026, and confirm it lands on 23 April 2026. Only day-values above 12 can distinguish the two readings.

Stuck?

Some rows come out as NaT. You're likely parsing with a single format. Parse each format separately and combine, or let the parser try both — but if you use an automatic parser, pin the day-first behaviour explicitly rather than letting it infer per row. Row-by-row inference is how you get April in one row and July in the next.

Everything parsed but my dates look wrong. Almost certainly day-first vs month-first. Check a value with a day above 12.

Task 2

Re-run the duplicate count and watch it move

  1. Count duplicates again on the normalized data.

    Why: same operation, same file, different comparison. Compare against the guess you wrote down in sub-task 8.

    expected output 
    duplicate grants
  before normalization (raw strings)      10
  after  normalization                    30
  ---------------------------------------------
  hidden duplicates revealed              20
  2. Verify the worked case: A000323.

    Why: this account belongs to jessica.schultz and has 12 raw rows covering 11 distinct grants. The extra is the Box / Member pair from the concept block above. After normalizing and de-duplicating you should have exactly 11 rows for this account, with exactly one Box / Member grant. The kit tests this account by name.

  3. Prove the ordering claim to yourself.

    Why: run it both ways. De-duplicate first, then normalize: you keep 15,178 rows. Normalize first, then de-duplicate: 15,158. Twenty format-twins survive the wrong order, and nothing anywhere in your pipeline will ever warn you about them.

Stuck?

I get 20, or 23, not 30. Check which columns you're comparing on. De-duplicating on (account_id, app, role) and de-duplicating on the full normalized row both give 30 in this data — but they're different questions, and the first is the one the ledger needs. Make sure you know which you asked.

Which of the two duplicate rows should I keep? In this data it doesn't matter — the twins are identical once normalized. Pick a deterministic rule anyway, such as earliest granted_date, and write it down. A pipeline whose output depends on row order is one you can't reproduce.

Task 3

Canonicalize department, and understand what it buys you

  1. Collapse the 32 spellings to the 12 real departments.

    Why: trim, then lowercase, then map to a canonical display form. Keep the raw value in a separate column rather than overwriting it — when a finding is questioned, you want to be able to show what the source system actually said.

    expected output 
    department spellings:  32 raw  ->  12 canonical
rows changed:          every row retains its original value in department_raw
  2. Understand precisely why this matters — and precisely how it would have fooled you.

    Why: in sub-task 12 a policy exemption applies only to a Controller in Finance & Accounting. It happens that all five accounts that exemption touches have a cleanly-cased department in this export, so an exact string match would have worked on those five rows.

    That is exactly what makes it dangerous. The bug is present, the test data doesn't trigger it, and it ships. Next quarter one of those people is recorded as FINANCE & ACCOUNTING, the exemption silently stops applying, and a Controller doing their job appears in a report as a critical finding. Normalizing isn't fixing a failure you can see — it's removing one you can't yet.

You're done when

You can show a before and an after, with the gap explained:

  • granted_date: 15188 / 15188 parsed to ISO, 0 failures, verified against a day-value above 12
  • Duplicates: 10 before normalization, 30 after — 20 hidden
  • Ordering proven both ways: 15,178 the wrong way, 15,158 the right way
  • A000323: 12 raw rows → 11 grants, one Box / Member
  • department: 32 → 12, with the raw value preserved

The transferable rule, which has nothing to do with Meridian: normalize, then compare. Every dataset you ever reconcile will have its own version of the July-versus-April problem.
