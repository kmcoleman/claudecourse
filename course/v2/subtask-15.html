Catch your agent citing a policy clause that doesn't exist

Depends on: Sub-task 14 and sub-task 6 — you need the structured call and failure mode three Time: ≈ 30 min Type: first agent · the motivation for Skills
First appearance
hallucinated citation grounding

Mode three from sub-task 6, met in the wild rather than provoked in a toy script.

In sub-task 6 you provoked three failure modes deliberately: a crash, a silent wrong answer, and a confident hallucinated rationale. Mode three was the one that needed a contrived setup to demonstrate, because it doesn't announce itself.

It doesn't need contriving here. Ask your agent to cite the policy clause behind its verdict — a completely reasonable request, the sort of thing an auditor asks as a matter of course — and it will produce one. Confident, well-formatted, in exactly the notation a real policy would use.

Then go and look for the clause.

What you'll understand and do

  • Provoke a hallucinated citation with an entirely reasonable request
  • Verify a citation the only way that counts — against the source file
  • State precisely why this happens, and why Skills are the answer

Concept

The one idea

The model has never read Meridian's policies. You never sent them.

This is the entire explanation, and it is worth sitting with because it is not what most people's intuition says. The model is not lying, malfunctioning, or being careless. It was asked for a citation, it has no access to the document, and it has read an enormous number of access-control policies during training. So it produces the shape of a plausible citation — and the shape is very convincing, because it learned that shape from real documents.

Why the fake citation is so persuasive

Real access policies do have clauses numbered like §4.2 or AC-7.1. Real dormancy standards do specify thresholds in days. A fabricated "Privileged Access Standard §3.2 requires review of privileged accounts inactive for more than 90 days" is wrong in every particular and reads exactly like a sentence from a genuine control document.

Which is why you cannot catch this by reading carefully. You catch it by checking.

Look at what Meridian's documents actually are, because the mismatch is instructive. There are five files in data/2026-Q3/policies/, and only access-control-policy.md has numbered clauses at all: ACP-1, ACP-4, ACP-4.1, ACP-4.2, ACP-6. That is the complete list. The other four — the SoD Policy, the Privileged Access Standard, the Contractor Access Standard, the Termination Procedure — have no internal numbering whatsoever and can only be cited by document title.

So any citation of the form "Privileged Access Standard §anything" is fabricated by construction. There is no section to number. You can prove that with grep in about four seconds, which is the point of this sub-task.

Why this is the last thing in Theme C

Because it is the argument for Theme D. Every fix you might reach for — a sterner prompt, a lower temperature, asking it to double-check — is a way of asking the model to be more careful about a document it has never seen. None of them can work.

The fix is to put the document in reach. That is what a Skill is, and it is building block number two.

Do this

Task 1

Ask for the citation

  1. Re-run the sub-task 14 call on A000012, asking it to populate policy_citations.

    Why: the field is required by the schema, so you may already have been getting one. If so, you have your material and can skip straight to checking it. Notice how ordinary the request is — "cite the policy" is the most reasonable thing an auditor can ask, and it is the thing that breaks.

  2. Write it down verbatim before you check it.

    Why: exact string. If you paraphrase you will unconsciously smooth it into something checkable, and the whole exercise turns on the fabrication being specific. Note the rationale too — that's usually where a fabricated threshold appears ("more than 90 days of inactivity") next to the fabricated clause.

Task 2

Go and look for it

  1. Grep the policy directory for the clause identifier.

    Why: this is the whole method, and its cheapness is the lesson. A citation you can't locate in the source file is not a weak citation — it is not a citation.

    terminal 
    grep -rn "3.2" data/2026-Q3/policies/       # whatever it actually cited
grep -rnoE "[A-Z]{2,4}-[0-9]+(\.[0-9]+)?|§[0-9.]+" data/2026-Q3/policies/

    The second command enumerates every clause-shaped token in all five documents. The complete output is ACP-1, ACP-4, ACP-4.1, ACP-4.2, ACP-6 — all from access-control-policy.md, and nothing from the other four files. That is the entire citable surface of Meridian's policy set.

  2. Read the real Privileged Access Standard. It is two sentences.

    Why: the gap between what the model asserted and what the document says is the finding. The real standard sets 180 days, not 90; it carries two requirements, not one (an approved ticket before the grant, and activity within 180 days); and it has a break-glass carve-out that the fabricated version knew nothing about. A verdict built on the invented version can be wrong in both directions.

    terminal 
    cat data/2026-Q3/policies/privileged-access-standard.md
  3. Try to fix it with prompting, and watch it not work.

    Why: worth thirty seconds, because it kills the instinct permanently. Add "only cite clauses that actually exist; if unsure, say so" and re-run. You'll get one of two outcomes, and both make the same point. Either it hedges into uselessness — no citation at all, so the finding is no longer audit-defensible — or it produces a different fabricated citation with more hedging attached. Neither is grounding. You cannot instruct your way to a document the model doesn't have.

  4. Write the lesson down while the sting is fresh.

    Why: this note is the reason the next four sub-tasks exist. It is also, in a real engagement, the slide that gets you the budget for doing grounding properly.

Stuck?

It cited ACP-4.2 and that one is real. Genuinely possible — it's the most-discussed clause in the dataset and the model may have picked it up from your own dossier or system prompt. Two things to do. First, check whether it's relevant: ACP-4.2 is the SoD Controller exemption and has nothing to do with a dormant Vault admin, so a correct clause number attached to the wrong control is its own category of wrong. Second, ask about a different control — contractor access, or the ticket requirement — and the fabrication reappears.

It refused to cite anything. Then you've hit the other failure mode, and it's arguably worse: a finding with no citation isn't audit-defensible, so the pipeline produces nothing usable. Record it as the outcome; it makes the same argument.

Should I just paste the policies into the prompt? That is a real fix and it would work here. It doesn't scale — five documents today, forty in a real engagement, and every call pays for all of them whether relevant or not. Sub-task 16 starts on the version that does scale.

You're done when

  • The real Privileged Access Standard read, and the gap between it and the invention written down
  • One sentence you'd say to a client about why prompting can't fix this

Theme C is complete. You have an agent: it takes a dossier, produces a schema-valid finding, and reports what it cost. You also have proof that its reasoning is ungrounded — well-formed, confident, and citing a document it has never read.
