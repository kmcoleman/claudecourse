Route by model tier for cost

Depends on: Sub-task 34 — you need the per-component cost breakdown Time: ≈ 45 min Type: evals · tiering
First appearance
model tiering capability/cost curve

The first optimisation with a quality risk attached — which is why you built the evals first.

Your pipeline has been running one model for everything: gathering facts, applying policy, writing records, judging rationales. Those are not equally hard jobs, and they don't need equally capable models.

Tiering is the cheapest structural cost lever available — a configuration change, not a rewrite. It is also the first one that can silently make your findings worse, which is why it comes after five evaluations rather than before them.

What you'll understand and do

  • Explain the capability/cost curve and where each component sits on it
  • Move one step to a cheaper tier
  • Measure the delta on cost and on quality, using the evals you built
  • Decide, with evidence, whether to keep it

Concept

The one idea

Match the model to the difficulty of the step, not to the difficulty of the project.

Models come in tiers. Roughly: Haiku for fast, simple, high-volume work; Sonnet for the balanced middle where most judgment lives; Opus for the hardest reasoning. Cost differs by a large multiple end to end, and so does capability — which is the part people forget when they see the price difference.

Where your components sit

ledger-reader — call seven tools, assemble the results, report them. Mechanical. Almost certainly fine on the cheapest tier, and it is also the component sub-task 34 probably showed you dominates the cost, because of the tool-use loop.

policy-applier — read ambiguous prose, apply it to a specific case, decide whether a scoped exemption reaches. This is the hard part of the system and the last place to economise.

findings-writer — fill a schema from an adjudication. Mechanical. Cheap tier.

The judge — apply a rubric to an artifact. Middling. Worth testing.

Notice that the expensive component and the hard component are different. That's what makes tiering worth doing here rather than a rounding error.

The risk is not that a cheaper model fails loudly. It's that ledger-reader on a cheap tier stops calling get_prior_review because it didn't occur to it, and the facts arrive slightly thinner. policy-applier then adjudicates competently on incomplete input and produces a defensible-looking finding that's missing something. Nothing errors. Nothing looks wrong.

Which is exactly the failure your evals are built to catch: the micro-key sees a changed verdict, the judge sees a thinner evidence array, and the coverage check sees a distribution shift. This is the sub-task where the previous six pay for themselves.

One practical wrinkle

Prompt caching has a minimum cacheable prefix, and it differs by model — higher on the cheapest tier than on the mid one. A Skills prefix that caches on Sonnet may be below the threshold on Haiku, so the cheaper model can lose you a discount you were relying on. Worth knowing before sub-task 36, and worth measuring rather than assuming.

Do this

Task 1

Move one step

  1. Pick the component with the best ratio of cost to difficulty.

    Why: sub-task 34 told you which one dominates. If it's ledger-reader — which it usually is — that's also one of the two most mechanical components, which makes it the obvious first move. Change one thing so the delta is attributable.

  2. Set the model in the agent's frontmatter and re-run.

    Why: a subagent file takes a model: field, so this is a one-line change. Use claude-haiku-4-5 for the cheap tier; claude-sonnet-5 is your current judgment default and claude-opus-5 is the step up if you want to test the other direction.

    Run at limit=20 against your stratified debug set. You want a comparison, not another full-quarter bill.

  3. Measure the cost delta honestly.

    Why: the per-token saving is not the whole story. A cheaper model may need more tool-use iterations to reach the same picture, and more iterations at a lower rate can cost more than fewer at a higher one. Compare total run cost, not unit price.

Task 2

Measure the quality delta — three ways

  1. Score against the micro-key.

    Why: the first and sharpest test. Did any of the twelve verdicts change? A single flipped is_finding is a real regression and probably decides this on its own.

  2. Run the judge.

    Why: this catches the subtle version. Even with identical verdicts, the evidence arrays may be thinner and the rationales less grounded. Compare the per-criterion distributions rather than a mean — a drop concentrated in evidence_grounding tells you exactly what the cheaper model stopped doing.

  3. Run the coverage checks and the self-consistency diff.

    Why: the distribution may have shifted without any individual finding looking wrong. And a cheaper model is often less stable, so run twice and compare the decision-level difference count against your sub-task 30 baseline. Instability introduced by a cost optimisation is a cost you're paying in a different currency.

  4. Decide, and write down the basis.

    Why: the decision is a trade, and the point of this sub-task is that you can now state both sides of it in numbers. If cost fell meaningfully and no quality measure moved, keep it. If any verdict changed, revert — the saving is not worth an unexplained flip. If cost fell and defensibility fell slightly, that's a genuine judgment call and it belongs in the write-up rather than in your head.

Stuck?

The cheaper model was just as good. Then keep it, and try the next component. That's the process working — and it's worth also trying the other direction on policy-applier: does a more capable model on the hard step improve the micro-key score? If it does, you've found a place to spend the savings.

Cost went up. Genuinely possible, and it's the interesting result. More iterations at a lower rate can exceed fewer at a higher one, and it's why "cheaper model" and "cheaper run" are different claims. Report it.

Should I tier per-account instead of per-component? A real pattern — cheap triage for the obvious cases, escalate the hard ones to a better model. It's more machinery than this sub-task needs, and your sub-task 32 escalation triggers are already most of the routing logic if you want to build it.

You're done when
  • Two runs, two model configurations, one variable changed
  • A cost delta measured on total run cost rather than unit price
  • A quality delta measured three ways — micro-key, judge, coverage and stability
  • A keep-or-revert decision with the deciding number named

You have made a cost decision on evidence rather than instinct. That is only possible because sub-tasks 29–33 exist, which is the argument for building evals before optimisations.

If you're curious

  • Try policy-applier on a more capable tier and score it. If the micro-key improves, the right move may be spending more on the hard step and less on the easy ones — which is what tiering is actually for, rather than uniform cost-cutting.
