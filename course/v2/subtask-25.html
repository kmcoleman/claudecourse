The first complete quarter. Two runs, two very different costs, one findings file.

Five accounts work. There are 166 in your candidate set, covering 1,919 grants that no rule could settle.

Scaling from five to 166 is not a matter of changing a number. Iterating at full scale is slow and expensive enough that you stop iterating, which is how a small bug survives twenty runs. The limit parameter exists to prevent exactly that, and it has been in the frozen contract since before you had anything to limit.

Two runs today. One small, to get it right. One complete, to find out what a quarter costs.

What you'll understand and do

  • Use limit as the iteration lever it was designed to be
  • Tune at 20 until the pipeline is stable, then run the full quarter
  • Produce a complete findings.json for 2026-Q3
  • Record two costs and two wall-clock times, and explain the ratio

Concept

The one idea

The cost of iterating is a design parameter. Set it deliberately.

Every debugging loop has a cycle time. Under a few seconds you experiment freely. At a minute you think before running. At ten minutes and real money you stop running, start reasoning about what probably went wrong, and ship a fix you didn't test.

That last state is where agentic systems go to die, and it isn't a discipline problem — it's an economics problem. The fix is to make the loop cheap by construction.

limit, in the contract since sub-task 12

run_review(export_dir: Path, limit: int | None = None) -> list[dict]

It caps how many candidates reach the agent layer. At limit=20 you get a run in a fraction of the time for a fraction of the cost. Grading calls it with None.

It was in the signature before you had an agent layer to limit. That is what a well-designed contract looks like — the affordance is there before you need it.

What a limited run does and doesn't tell you. It tells you the pipeline runs, the handoffs work, the output validates, and roughly what a judgment costs. It does not tell you the findings are complete — twenty accounts out of 166 will not contain all nine findings, and a run that produces four findings at limit=20 is not evidence of anything being wrong.

Which twenty? Genuinely a decision. The first twenty in whatever order your candidate set happens to be in is easy and unrepresentative — it may contain no contractors at all, and you'd never exercise that path. A stratified twenty covering each finding category is more work and much more useful. Take a moment on it; a debug set that doesn't cover your cases is a debug loop that lies.

Two numbers to have before you scale

From sub-task 22 you know roughly what one account costs in a tool-use loop. From this sub-task you'll know what 166 cost. The ratio should be near-linear — if it isn't, something is accumulating that shouldn't be, and finding out at 166 is much better than finding out at 1,200.

Task 1

Iterate at twenty

  1. Choose your twenty deliberately.

    Why: you want at least one of each finding category and at least one exemption, or the limited run can't tell you whether those paths work. Include A000012, A000384, A001115, A001185 and A000387 from sub-task 24, plus a spread of ordinary accounts that should produce nothing at all — the no-finding path is a path too, and a pipeline that flags everything passes a test set of only true positives.

  2. Run at limit=20 and record time and cost.

    Why: baseline both. You'll compare against the full run, and you'll want them again in sub-task 34.

    terminal 
    time python -m meridian_capstone.contract.run_review data/2026-Q3 --limit 20
  3. Iterate here until it's boring.

    Why: this is where the sub-task's time actually goes, and it should. Fix the schema violations, the handoff losses, the citations that don't resolve, the exemptions that got dropped instead of recorded. Every one of those found at 20 is one you don't pay 166 accounts to find.

    The bar for moving on: two consecutive runs where the output validates, every citation resolves against your sub-task 16 inventory, and the accounts you know about come out right. Not "it worked once".

Stuck?

My limited run finds fewer findings than expected. Check whether the accounts you expected are actually in the twenty. limit caps the candidates, and if your ordering excludes the interesting ones you're testing the boring path.

It's still slow at 20. Look at calls per account. Three agents each running a tool-use loop can be a lot of model calls; twelve per account is 240 for the run. Either tighten what each specialist gathers, or make the fan-out concurrent.

Results differ between runs at the same limit. Expected, and it's the subject of sub-task 30. For now note how much they differ — if the same account flips between finding and no-finding, that is a much bigger problem than rationale wording changing, and it's worth chasing now.

Stuck?

The full run found more than 9 findings. Most likely duplicates — the same account flagged twice, once per grant. Findings are per account per category. A close second is an exemption that wasn't applied; check the three Controllers first.

Fewer than 9. Which category is short? Missing a contractor is usually A000686, Jamie Alvarez's second account, which sub-task 18 warned about. Missing a dormant admin usually means the as-of date drifted.

It rate-limited partway through. Real constraint at this scale. Back off and retry, or reduce concurrency. Also worth noting as an operational fact — sub-task 36's batch run is partly an answer to this.

It's expensive and I don't want to run it twice. Reasonable. You need one clean full run for this sub-task; everything after it should go back to limit=20 until sub-task 36 makes the full run cheap.
