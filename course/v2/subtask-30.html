Run twice and diff — instability is an underspecified prompt

Depends on: Sub-task 29 — you need the validator and a baseline distribution Time: ≈ 60 min Type: evals · self-consistency
First appearance
self-consistency paraphrase test

The second eval, and the one that most often produces an immediate concrete fix.

Run your pipeline twice on the same export. You will not get the same output. Rationales will be worded differently, confidence values will move, and if you are unlucky an account will appear in one run and not the other.

The first two are cosmetic. The third is a defect, and the useful thing about it is where the defect is. It is almost never in the model. It is in a prompt or a Skill that left a decision genuinely open, so the model made it differently twice.

Divergence is not discovery. It's a specification bug with a very precise address.

What you'll understand and do

  • Measure self-consistency across identical runs
  • Separate cosmetic variation from decision variation
  • Run the paraphrase test as a second, harder stability check
  • Tighten one prompt or Skill because of what you found

Concept

The one idea

Where the model wobbles, the specification is silent.

Model calls are non-deterministic. Given identical input the output distribution is not a point. That is a property of the technology, not a fault in your system, and no amount of prompt engineering removes it entirely.

But the size of the variation is under your control, and it is diagnostic. Where your specification is tight, runs agree. Where it's loose, they diverge — and the divergence points straight at the loose part.

Three tiers of variation

Cosmetic. The rationale is worded differently, the evidence entries are ordered differently. Ignore it — the finding is the same finding.

Graded. Confidence moves from 0.8 to 0.7; severity moves from high to medium. Worth watching. Small movement is fine; a wide spread on the same account means the Skill doesn't say how to grade.

Decision. An account is a finding in one run and not the other, or the category changes. This is a bug. It means the account sits on a boundary your specification never drew, and which side it lands on is a coin toss.

The paraphrase test is the harder version. Re-run with your prompts and Skills reworded — same meaning, different sentences. A system that survives identical runs but falls apart under paraphrase is one whose behaviour depends on the incidental phrasing of your instructions rather than on their content. That's brittle in a way that will bite the first time someone edits a Skill for clarity.

Where the instability will be

Not in the SoD findings. Those come from set containment against a matrix, and there is nothing to be uncertain about.

Look at the boundary cases. An account whose last login is close to the 180-day cutoff. A contractor whose ticket almost reads like a renewal. Someone in Finance & Accounting whose title is adjacent to Controller. Those are where a specification runs out, and they are where two runs will disagree.

Do this

Task 1

Run twice, diff properly

  1. Run the same export twice at limit=20 and save both.

    Why: no reason to pay for two full runs. Twenty accounts covering every category is enough to see the effect, as long as your debug set from sub-task 25 is the stratified one.

  2. Diff at the level that matters — decisions, not text.

    Why: a raw diff on two JSON files reports every reworded rationale and buries the one thing you care about. Compare the sets first, then the graded fields, and only then look at prose.

    the comparison you want 
    accounts flagged      set A vs set B — symmetric difference
category per account  same account, same category?
severity per account  same, or one step apart, or wildly different?
confidence per account  spread across runs
recommendation        revoke / review / retain — same?
exemptions            same accounts suppressed, same clause?
  3. Classify every difference into the three tiers.

    Why: the count per tier is the measurement. Zero decision-level differences is the target and it is achievable — most of the 2026-Q3 findings are unambiguous enough that a well-specified system gets them every time. One or two is a finding about your specification. Five is a system you cannot ship.

  4. Take one decision-level difference and find the silence.

    Why: this is the actual work. Read both rationales for that account side by side. They will usually be reasoning about the same fact and weighting it differently — which means your Skill states the rule but not how to apply it at the margin.

    If there are no decision-level differences, don't celebrate yet — go looking. Widen to more accounts, or run a third time. A system can be stable on twenty accounts and unstable on the twenty-first, and the boundary cases are rare by construction.

Stuck?

The runs are identical. Possible on twenty unambiguous accounts, and good news. Verify the two runs really were separate rather than one cached result, then push harder: run at a higher limit, or construct a boundary case — an account whose last login is a week either side of the cutoff — and run that ten times.

Everything differs. Look for a systematic cause before concluding the model is unstable. A different as-of date between runs, a Skill that didn't load the second time, or a partially-written findings file will all present as total divergence.

Should I chase the confidence spread? Only if it's wide. Confidence moving 0.8 to 0.75 is noise. The same account coming back 0.9 and 0.4 means your Skill gives no guidance on what confidence expresses, which is worth one sentence to fix.

Task 2

Paraphrase, then tighten

  1. Reword one Skill without changing its meaning, and run again.

    Why: pick the one governing the category where you saw the most variation. Rewrite the sentences — different structure, different vocabulary, same requirements and same exemptions. Then diff against both previous runs.

    Findings that survive rewording depend on what the Skill says. Findings that don't depend on how it happens to be phrased, and those are the fragile ones.

  2. Fix exactly one thing, and prove the fix.

    Why: one change, measured. Take the decision-level instability you located and add the sentence that resolves it — usually a tie-break rule, an explicit statement of what to do when evidence is absent rather than negative, or a definition of what confidence means here.

    Then run twice more and confirm that specific instability is gone. A fix you haven't re-measured is a hypothesis.

    Self-consistency report

    This is a real deliverable. On an engagement it goes in the methodology section, because a reviewer will ask whether the system says the same thing twice.

  3. Make the check repeatable, and commit.

    Why: a self-consistency check you can run on demand is worth having — it's the first thing to reach for after any prompt or Skill change. Script the two-run diff so it's one command.

Stuck?

My fix made it worse elsewhere. Genuinely common and worth sitting with. Tightening one boundary can shift behaviour on a neighbouring one, which is why sub-task 31's micro-key matters — it gives you a fixed target so you can tell "changed" from "regressed".

Can't I just reduce temperature? Some models expose sampling controls and some don't, and reaching for one here treats the symptom. If two runs disagree, a human reviewer given the same dossier could also disagree, and no sampling parameter resolves a question your specification never answered.

How stable is stable enough? Zero decision-level differences on accounts that matter; graded differences small and explicable; cosmetic differences ignored entirely. Anything short of that goes in your methodology section as a stated limitation rather than being quietly hoped about.

You're done when
  • Two identical runs diffed structurally, with differences counted by tier
  • At least one decision-level instability traced to a specific silence in a Skill or prompt — or a documented search that found none
  • A paraphrase run showing which findings depend on meaning and which on phrasing
  • One tightening made, and re-measured
  • The two-run diff scripted so it's one command

You now know whether your system agrees with itself. You still have no idea whether it agrees with a competent reviewer.

Sub-task 31 makes the only ground truth you're allowed to make.

If you're curious

  • Run the same single account ten times and plot the confidence values. The shape tells you something the mean doesn't — a tight cluster and a bimodal split are very different situations, and only one of them is noise.
