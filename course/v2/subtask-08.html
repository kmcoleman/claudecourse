Load the raw data and prove it has three planted messes

Depends on: Sub-task 7 — your rules are in CLAUDE.md, and the bench is green Time: ≈ 60 min Type: reconciliation · foundations
First appearance
pandas DataFrame seed lock

Theme A was about the tool. Theme B is about the data underneath it. Nothing here involves an agent — that's the point.

Before you can review Meridian's access, you have to be able to read Meridian's exports. That sounds trivial. It is not, and the ten minutes you spend proving it is the cheapest ten minutes in this course.

Both files here are real-shaped: the sort of thing a client actually sends when you ask for "the HR roster and the entitlements dump". They came out of two different systems, maintained by two different teams, with two different ideas about how to write a date. Three specific messes are planted in them. Your job in this sub-task is not to fix any of them — it's to measure all three, so that later, when a number looks wrong, you know whether the data or your code is lying.

What you'll understand and do

  • Load a CSV into a DataFrame and report its shape without printing it
  • Measure the three data-quality defects in the Q3 export, exactly
  • Explain why the export is seed-locked, and what that lets you assert

Concept

The one idea

Every number you report later is bounded by the data you didn't look at.

A user access review ends with a sentence like "we found 14 accounts that should be revoked." That sentence is only as good as the join underneath it. If 12% of accounts never matched a person, your 14 is really "14, plus whatever is hiding in the 12% I silently dropped."

Auditors do not accept that, and they are right not to. So the discipline is: measure the defects first, in the open, with numbers. Then every later claim can be stated with its own error bars attached.

What is pandas, and what is a DataFrame?

pandas is the standard Python library for tabular data. A DataFrame is its table type — rows, named columns, and a large set of operations that work on whole columns at once instead of one row at a time.

You will use perhaps eight of its features in this whole theme: read_csv, .shape, .columns, .nunique(), .value_counts(), .duplicated(), .merge(), and .to_csv(). You do not need to learn pandas. You need to be able to read what Claude writes with it and tell whether it's right.

The seed lock. The export in data/2026-Q3/ was generated from a fixed random seed. That means every number on this page is exact, not approximate, and it will be exact on your machine too. If your count differs by one, one of us is wrong — and the whole point of a seed lock is that you get to find out which.

Why this sub-task bans agents

You could ask Claude "what's wrong with this data?" and get a plausible essay. You would have no way to check it. Counting rows is not a judgment call, and anything that isn't a judgment call belongs in code — a principle you'll name explicitly in sub-task 11.

Get the two files into memory and report their shape

What it has to do: read data/2026-Q3/hr_roster.csv and data/2026-Q3/entitlements.csv with pandas, and print row and column counts for each, the column names, and a handful of distinct-value counts.     Why: these are exact. The export is seed-locked.

    expected output

    Two of those lines should already bother you. 1,200 accounts but only 1,188 distinct account names — so some name owns more than one account. And 1,190 people but 1,200 accounts — there are more accounts than humans. Both are sub-task 9's problem. Note them and move on.

FileNotFoundError. The paths above are relative to the repository root, the folder that contains pyproject.toml. Run the script from there, or have it resolve paths relative to its own location.

Claude printed the whole table anyway. Worth pausing on. You wrote a rule against exactly this in sub-task 7. Did it get followed? Rules are guidance, not enforcement — this is that lesson arriving with a concrete cost attached.

My numbers are close but not equal. Then something is wrong and it's worth finding. The most common cause is reading data/2026-Q4/, which also ships in this repo for a much later sub-task.

Task 2

Mess one — two date formats in a single column

  1. Measure how many granted_date values are ISO (YYYY-MM-DD) and how many are US (MM/DD/YYYY).

    Why: this column is roughly half and half, which is the worst possible case. A column that's 100% wrong gets noticed immediately. A column that's 50% wrong sorts, compares, and aggregates without ever raising an error — it just gives you the wrong answer quietly.

    Have your script classify each value by pattern and count both buckets. Don't parse yet; just classify.

    expected output 
    granted_date formats
  ISO  (YYYY-MM-DD)   7538
  US   (MM/DD/YYYY)   7650
  other                  0
  total              15188

hire_date formats
  ISO  (YYYY-MM-DD)   1190
  US   (MM/DD/YYYY)      0

    The contrast is the finding. hire_date in the HR export is perfectly clean; granted_date in the entitlements export is not. The mess is not "Meridian" — it's one system at Meridian. In a real engagement that sentence is what you put in the email.

  2. Write down, in one line, why 04/07/2025 is dangerous.

    Why: that value appears in row 2 of the file. It is April 7th, because this export is US-formatted. Read as day-first it becomes July 4th — a three-month error, in the direction that decides whether a grant predates someone's hire date. You will make this exact mistake in sub-task 10 if you don't decide the convention now.

Task 3

Mess two — one department, many spellings

  1. Count the distinct raw values in HR's department column, then count them again after trimming whitespace and lowercasing.

    Why: this is the cheapest possible demonstration that "distinct" is a claim about your comparison function, not about the world.

    expected output 
    department
  raw distinct values             32
  after strip + lowercase         12

    Twelve real departments, wearing thirty-two costumes. Operations alone appears as Operations, operations, OPERATIONS, and " Operations " with padding spaces.

  2. Print the twelve canonical departments and keep the list.

    Why: you'll need it in sub-task 12, where a policy exemption applies only to people in Finance & Accounting. An exact string comparison against a column with 32 spellings is a bug waiting for the right row to land on.

Stuck?

I get 31, or 33. Check whether you're trimming before counting the raw values — you shouldn't be. The raw count includes the padded variants like " Engineering "; that's the whole point of the number.

Should I fix these now? No. Measure in this sub-task, normalize in sub-task 10. Separating the two is what lets you show a before and an after.

Task 4

Mess three — rows that are byte-for-byte identical

  1. Count entitlement rows that duplicate an earlier row exactly, across all seven columns.

    Why: exact duplicates are the easy half of a problem you'll meet properly in sub-task 10. Getting the easy number now makes the hard number legible later.

    expected output 
    entitlements exact duplicate rows: 10
                  unique rows: 15178
  2. Ask yourself the question that matters, and don't answer it yet.

    Why: ten looks like a small, tidy problem. Hold on to that impression. In sub-task 10 you'll normalize the dates and count again, and the number will change in a way that reframes what "duplicate" means. Guess now, in your notes, what it will change to. Being wrong is more instructive than being right.

  3. Commit your progress.

    pytest -m "not api"
git add explore.py
git commit -m "Add explore.py: measure the three defects in the Q3 export"

    Still 33 passed, 11 skipped, 1 deselected. You haven't produced any artifact the tests are waiting for — that starts in sub-task 9.

You're done when

explore.py runs and prints numbers that match these exactly:

  • hr_roster.csv 1190 × 9; entitlements.csv 15188 × 7
  • 1200 distinct account_id but only 1188 distinct account_name
  • granted_date: 7538 ISO + 7650 US = 15188, with 0 unclassified
  • department: 32 raw spellings → 12 real departments
  • 10 exact duplicate rows

You now know three things that are wrong with this data, with exact counts for each. Every claim you make for the rest of the course inherits those numbers — which is precisely why you measured them before touching anything.
